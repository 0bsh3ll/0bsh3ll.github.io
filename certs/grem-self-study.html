How I Passed GREM in a Month with Self-Study

📅 2026-10-09 ⏱ 7 min read 🛡 GIAC GREM
I’m a Developer turned security analyst. I cleared the GIAC Reverse Engineering Malware (GREM) exam with 87% after roughly a month of preparation. I used a lot of free online material, one optional paid course that sped things up, 2 GIAC practice tests, 1 exam attempt and a gallon of black tea. This is the place I spill the tea on how I could achieve this in a month with the step-by-step method I followed and every resource used, for anyone looking for a place to start.

01 — Why GREM

I'm a developer turned security analyst. While working as an analyst, I found malware analysis to be a really cool topic. Understanding how malware works and learning to identify it felt genuinely interesting, so I started spending extra time after my shifts digging deeper. GREM stood out as a good certification to cement that understanding.

02 — Where I Started

I wouldn't say it was all free. To feed my curiosity, I bought the Malware Reverse Engineering & Detection Engineering course from Intelliroot Academy. It gave me a strong foundation.

ℹ️ Why self-study? Preparing with the official SANS course (FOR610) is a lot easier, but I couldn't afford it. This exam was self-funded, so on my budget I made the choice to go the self-study route. The Intelliroot course helped me speed up my learning, and I recommend it because it teaches a lot more than what's needed for the GREM exam. Still, you can do all of this with just the free materials I've listed in this post.

Even before that, I found some awesome free content that gave me a good grasp of assembly and the stack. Those links are under Fundamentals in 04 — Online Resources.

03 — My Method, Step by Step

1. Know exactly what the exam covers

The first objective is to understand what you need to learn for the exam. GIAC states clearly what you need to be good at. Go to the GREM certification page and open the Objectives tab.

2. Find materials and tools for each objective

Next, identify the materials and tools you can use to learn each objective. I've listed the ones I used in 04 — Online Resources.

3. Build an isolated lab

Never analyse malware on your own machine. Build an isolated lab first. This video is a good place to start. While you set it up, pick your disassembler and debugger (see Tools in 04).

4. Collect samples and put in the hours

Look for malware on MalwareBazaar and the other sources below, and find samples from every category in the objectives. There's no easy way here: you have to put in the time. My course helped me get some samples, but you can easily get them from the sites I've listed.

5. Take your first practice test

Once you start getting comfortable, buy a practice test from GIAC. It shows you how they ask questions and how much time you get. It also tells you which disassemblers, debuggers and other tools the lab environment supports. I failed my first one with 56%, but it showed me my weaknesses and the types of questions they ask.

6. Fix your weak spots

Use those tools and prepare again. If you don't understand something or get stuck, GenAI, YouTube and Reddit become your best friends. This is also when you build your index (see 05).

⚠️ Use GenAI only to learn concepts. Never paste GIAC exam or practice test questions into ChatGPT or any other AI tool, forum or study site. It's against the GIAC Exam Integrity Policy, and it can get your certification revoked.

7. Take the second practice test

When you're ready, attempt the next practice test. A score of 85+ is a good sign that you're ready. I scored 99% on my second one. I was more than thrilled.

8. One more week, then the real thing

One more week of practice, and then I sat the GREM exam.

04 — Online Resources

Everything below is available online. Most of it is free.

Fundamentals & practice

  • 0xinfection — Reversing: assembly
  • Phrack 49 — Smashing the Stack for Fun and Profit: the stack
  • crackmes.one: practice binaries to reverse

Malware samples

  • MalwareBazaar
  • MalShare
  • VirusShare: a good collection of samples that are hard to find elsewhere (registration required)

Tools

  • Office documents: most of the tools you need are on Didier Stevens' blog
  • Disassemblers: Binary Ninja, IDA or Ghidra
  • Debuggers: WinDbg, OllyDbg or x64dbg

Pick a disassembler and a debugger from these when you set up your lab.

Lab

  • Building an isolated analysis lab (video)
05 — Building the Index
💡 When you build your index, order it by the GIAC objectives. In the exam, you'll know exactly where to look.

I struggled to remember the flags for each tool, so I printed the help and usage pages for all of them and brought them along with my index. I was even crazy enough to print all the Windows APIs for the exam.

06 — The Practice Tests
ℹ️ Exam format (at the time of writing): 66 questions in 3 hours, including hands-on CyberLive lab questions. The labs take much longer than the multiple-choice questions, so time is of the essence and you really have to conserve it.

Practice test 1, 56% (failed): this showed me my weaknesses, the types of questions they ask, and which tools the lab environment supports.

Practice test 2, 99%: after fixing my weak spots, I was confident. If you're scoring 85+, you're ready.

07 — Exam Day Tips

The real exam was a bit more confusing than the practice tests.

💡 When in doubt, skip it. I skipped the questions I wasn't sure about and came back to them near the end of the exam. It really saves time, and otherwise you get stuck. Highly recommended.
08 — Result

Passed with 87%. The whole thing took roughly a month.

This note is for anyone looking for a place to start preparing for GREM, or any other exam. It's a method that's tried, tested and works.

Reach out to me any time you're struggling, on LinkedIn or GitHub. A lot of people helped me on this journey, and I'd like to return the favour.
